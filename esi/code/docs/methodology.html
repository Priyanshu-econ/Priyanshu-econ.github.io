1. Purpose and scope

The Energy Security Index (ESI) measures how secure a country's energy system is, on a scale from 0 to 1, for 40 countries over 2000–2024 (a balanced panel of 1,000 country-years). It was constructed for the author's job market paper, where it is the dependent variable in quantile, IV-GMM and logit models of the drivers of energy security, and is released here so that others can use it. This document records every construction choice and the reason for it, following the ten-step guidance of the OECD and JRC handbook on composite indicators (OECD, 2008).

2. Conceptual framework: the four As

The index follows the widely used 4-A framework (APERC, 2007; Kruyt et al., 2009; Ang et al., 2015), which treats energy security as having four dimensions:

  • Availability: whether enough energy is physically available, and how much of it is produced at home.
  • Accessibility: whether people and firms can actually reach that energy through reliable networks.
  • Affordability: whether energy can be paid for, given its price and households' purchasing power.
  • Acceptability: whether energy is used in an environmentally sustainable and efficient way.

Each dimension is measured by two indicators, eight in total. The framework has known limits, notably that it says little about how quickly an energy system recovers from a shock (Cherp & Jewell, 2014), but it remains the most common basis for cross-country energy security indices.

3. Countries and period

The sample is 40 economies chosen for data coverage across all eight indicators and the explanatory variables of the paper: Argentina, Australia, Belgium, Brazil, Canada, Chile, China, Colombia, Denmark, Egypt, Finland, France, Germany, Hungary, India, Indonesia, Israel, Italy, Japan, Malaysia, Mexico, Netherlands, Norway, Peru, Philippines, Poland, Portugal, Russia, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, South Korea, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Thailand, Türkiye, Ukraine, United Kingdom, United States, Venezuela. The period is 2000–2024, the longest span for which all indicators are available for every country.

Missing values

All raw values were taken from the source files and matched to countries by ISO 3166 code. Where a source had no value for a country-year (in total 3.0% of indicator values), the gap was filled by linear interpolation between observed years, or, at the start or end of a series, by carrying the nearest observed value forward or back. Every filled value is flagged in the data (variables ending in _filled), so users can drop or re-impute them.

5. Normalisation: pooled min–max

The eight indicators are measured in different units, so each is rescaled to a common 0–1 scale with the min–max method. Indicators where a higher value means more security use Eq. (1); indicators where a lower value means more security use Eq. (2), so that 1 is always the most secure value:

pit = (xit − min x) / (max x − min x)   (1)      pit = (max x − xit) / (max x − min x)   (2)

Why min–max. It gives bounded, non-negative scores, which the geometric aggregation in Section 7 requires (z-scores can be negative and cannot enter a geometric mean). It keeps the relative distances between observations, and it is the method used in established indices such as the Human Development Index (UNDP, 2025).

Why pooled. The minimum and maximum are taken over all countries and all years together, not year by year. A score of 0.6 therefore means the same thing in 2000 and in 2024, and changes over time reflect real changes in the indicators rather than changes in the yearly best and worst performers.

What it implies. Scores are relative to this sample: 1 is the best value observed among these 40 countries over 2000–2024, not an absolute target, and adding countries or years would change the scores. Min–max is also sensitive to extreme values, which is why energy CPI inflation is treated first (next paragraph).

Outlier treatment: energy CPI inflation. Energy CPI inflation is extremely skewed (skewness 30.2, kurtosis 934) because of hyperinflation episodes, above all Venezuela in 2018 (94,802%). Left untreated, that single value sets the maximum, 99.7% of country-years score above 0.99, and the indicator stops distinguishing countries. Extreme values can become unintended benchmarks under min–max (OECD, 2008), and the JRC statistical audits of composite indicators treat an indicator when its absolute skewness exceeds 2.25 and its kurtosis exceeds 3.5 (Saisana et al., 2018). Energy CPI inflation is therefore winsorised at its pooled 1st and 99th percentiles (-8.59% and 71.81%; 20 of 1,000 values) before normalisation. After treatment 1.5% of country-years score above 0.99. No other indicator is treated. The raw values are kept in the data files, next to the winsorised series used for the score.

6. Weighting: equal weights

Each of the four dimensions receives a weight of 0.25, and the two indicators within each dimension receive 0.5 each, so every indicator carries an overall weight of 0.125 (12.5%). Equal weighting is the standard choice when neither theory nor evidence gives a clear reason to rank one dimension above another (OECD, 2008), and it keeps the index transparent. Section 8 shows how much the results depend on this choice.

7. Aggregation: arithmetic within, geometric across

The score of dimension d for country i in year t is the arithmetic mean of its two normalised indicators, and the ESI is the geometric mean of the four dimension scores:

Ddit = ½ (pit(d,1) + pit(d,2)),     ESIit = (DAvail · DAccess · DAfford · DAccept)1/4   (3)

Why this combination. Within a dimension the two indicators measure the same aspect of security, so letting them offset each other is reasonable. Across dimensions, full offsetting is not: a country with plentiful but unaffordable energy is not secure. The geometric mean limits this compensation, because a low score in one dimension pulls the whole index down and can only be partly offset by high scores elsewhere (OECD, 2008; Chentouf & Allouch, 2022). The index therefore rewards balanced energy systems. A dimension score of exactly zero would set the ESI to zero; no dimension reaches zero in this sample (the lowest is 0.007, for Availability).

8. Sensitivity and robustness

Weights. The ESI was recomputed 1,000 times with random dimension weights (drawn uniformly and rescaled to sum to one; random seed 123). The mean of the simulated index is 0.547 against 0.532 for the baseline. Across all country-years the average country moves 0.56 places in the yearly ranking (standard deviation 0.76, at most 4 places), so the rankings do not hinge on equal weighting. The 5th–95th percentile range of the simulated index is reported for every country-year in the sensitivity file and shown as a band on the website.

Aggregation. An arithmetic mean of the four dimensions correlates 0.76 with the geometric ESI (Spearman rank correlation 0.69). The difference is expected: the arithmetic mean lets strong Accessibility and Affordability scores offset weak Availability, which is precisely the compensation the geometric mean is designed to limit. With z-score normalisation in place of min–max (each dimension rescaled to [0, 1] before the geometric mean), the index correlates 0.95 with the baseline (Spearman 0.95). With weights taken from a principal component analysis of the four dimensions, the correlation is 0.86 (Spearman 0.85).

Use in the paper. In the accompanying paper the ESI is the dependent variable. Geopolitical risk is the country-specific index of Caldara and Iacoviello (2022), standardised within each country (its deviation from the country's 2000–2024 mean, divided by the country's standard deviation), because the raw levels depend on international press coverage and are not comparable across countries. A low- and high-security regime is defined by whether the ESI is above its pooled median.
